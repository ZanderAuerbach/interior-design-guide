Studio 01

Axel Vervoordt

Antwerp · Wijnegem · S'Gravenwezel

Practice Overview

Wabi & Timelessness

Axel Vervoordt Company has operated from the Castle of 't Steen near Antwerp for decades, producing interiors that translate Japanese wabi-sabi philosophy into the European context. The aesthetic is one of disciplined restraint: raw plaster, aged timber, natural stone, linen, and hand-thrown ceramics — all in a palette of ash, straw, and faded earth. Antiques and contemporary art sit alongside each other without hierarchy, unified by the quality of material and the hand of time.

Vervoordt is also a significant gallerist and furniture dealer; the boundary between the shop, the showroom, and the design practice is deliberately porous. Hospitality commissions (Les Ottomans Istanbul, RFH Le Royal Monceau Paris) demonstrate that the approach scales to the institutional. The annual TEFAF booth has become a defining reference for how antique and contemporary objects can coexist without apology. His son Boris now leads the firm.

Key references: Wabi-sabi · Mingei movement · Axel's own art dealing practice · Mies van der Rohe restraint

Studio 02

Vincent Van Duysen

Antwerp, Belgium

Practice Overview

Serene Materiality

Vincent Van Duysen Architects produces what is arguably the most refined residential architecture-and-interiors practice working in Belgium today. His projects — often in converted farmhouses, industrial buildings, or newly built villas — are defined by monolithic calm: plaster, stone, concrete, linen, and dark-stained timber deployed in massive, unhurried compositions. Light enters in long horizontal bands; surfaces wear their age; rooms breathe.

Van Duysen was creative director of Molteni&C (2016–22), translating the studio aesthetic into furniture at scale. The furniture is as important as the architecture: his pieces for Molteni, RH, and various collaborators share the same interest in mass, proportion, and material patina. A project by VVD invariably uses his own furniture alongside antiques — a bed frame in oxidised brass, a sofa in undyed linen, a dining table in solid travertine — making the whole indivisible from the parts.

Key references: Minimalism with warmth · Belgian vernacular · John Pawson discipline · Axel Vervoordt restraint · Molteni&C collaboration

03

Rose Uniacke

London, Pimlico  ·  roseuniacke.com

Rose Uniacke is among the most consequential British interior designers of the last twenty years — a figure who has largely redefined what domestic elegance looks like in the contemporary era. Her signature is "sumptuous minimalism": period rooms approached with a pared-back choreography, where antiques from antiquity to the 20th century live alongside bespoke contemporary pieces and textiles. Light and proportion are paramount. Art — often by female artists working in textile, ceramics, and paper — is integral, not decorative afterthought. Her own fabric and paint lines extend the practice into a complete material ecosystem. Recent work includes London townhouses, a New York hotel (San Vicente West Village), the Belmond Royal Scotsman, and her personal Coach House — photographed by Francois Halard for World of Interiors.

Interior Design · Fabric · Paint · Antiques

San Vicente West Village  ·  New York  ·  Photography: Simon Upton

A Place with a Colorful History

A comprehensive interior renovation of a 1908 redbrick building in Manhattan's West Village — once part of the Jane Hotel, originally lodgings for visiting sailors. Rose Uniacke transformed it into a members' club and hotel for Jeff Klein: a quiet, layered sophistication grounded in the building's bones. As written in Architectural Digest: "Walking into a room by Rose feels like walking into the best version of the home you wish you owned."

04

Studioilse

London  ·  Ilse Crawford

Ilse Crawford is among the most original design thinkers of her generation — a studio that consistently asks what spaces are for, how they feel to inhabit, and what it means to be human within them. The work moves across scales and typologies: private homes, floating residences, rural coaching inns, Stockholm guest houses. What binds them is a commitment to sensory intelligence over visual novelty, and a deep literacy in materials, craft, and the domestic arts. She is equally at home with hand-stitched textiles and structural timber as with contemporary furniture or hand-painted antiques. Old and new in conversation, never in conflict.

Interior Design · Architecture · Strategy

Chalet, Switzerland

Switzerland  ·  2015  ·  Photography: Felix Odell

A family mountain home buried deep in the Swiss Alps. Working from a warm palette of natural materials — pitched timber, stone, hand-stitched textiles — Studioilse created a chalet that can adapt from festive gathering to quiet hibernation. A hand-painted botanical chest in the bedroom demonstrates the studio's belief in the singular object: one piece that anchors an entire room and earns its place through character, not price.

Chalet, Swiss Alps, 2015  ·  Photography: Felix Odell  ·  studioilse.com →

Shemara, Motor Yacht

Mallorca  ·  2015  ·  Photography: Casper Sejersen

The refurbishment of a historic motor yacht originally built in 1938. Studioilse's brief was to create a floating family home that made sense for both owners and crew — evoking the glamorous spirit of the boat's extraordinary history without becoming a slave to it. The result is a yacht interior that feels genuinely domestic: proper rooms, not cabins. The Shemara's story includes the Titanic; its interior should acknowledge history without pastiche.

Shemara, Motor Yacht, 2015  ·  Photography: Casper Sejersen  ·  studioilse.com →

Ett Hem, Number 4

Stockholm, Sweden  ·  2022  ·  Photography: Magnus Marding

A decade after Studioilse helped define a new language of hospitality with the original Ett Hem, they returned to expand the Stockholm guest house into the house next door. The same values, a new building: intimate and lively, domestic and public at once. Generous light from large windows opens onto rooftop views. Food and the local supply chain are foregrounded. The rooms are expansive but never grand — they feel like somewhere you might actually want to live.

The Olde Bell Inn

Hurley, Berkshire  ·  2007  ·  Photography: Lisa Cohen

A benchmark project: the contemporary reinterpretation of a traditional English coaching inn. Forty-seven bedrooms, a dining room, bar, malt house, and meadow garden — all reinterpreted using British materials, furniture, and local produce. The result is a rural hospitality model rooted in local life, not heritage pastiche. Old and new placed confidently side by side: exposed beams meet contemporary upholstery; antique mirrors hold company with clean-lined lighting. Craft, not costume.

Studio 05

Joseph Dirand

Paris

Practice Overview

Haussmann Rigour

Joseph Dirand Architecture operates in the gap between interior design and architecture — each project begins from structure, moves through proportion, and arrives at an interior of almost Baroque control. Parisian apartments are the central subject: enfilades of white rooms with high moulded ceilings, chevron parquet floors, and an extremely spare selection of objects chosen for sculptural silhouette rather than period provenance.

The palette is rigorously restrained — usually warm white or off-white walls, honey-toned stone, bleached or dark-stained wood — but the detail work is elaborate: bespoke joinery, custom light fixtures in plaster or blackened steel, upholstery in Dedar or Hermès fabrics. Hospitality projects (Monsieur Bleu Paris, Girafe Paris, Cecconi's) prove the approach can fill a very large room without losing intimacy. High reference for anyone working on Haussmann-era renovations.

Key references: French classical proportion · Surrealist object · Pierre Paulin furniture · Paris apartment tradition

Elliott Architects

Blue Hill, Maine  ·  Matthew Elliott

On the Maine coast, Elliott Architects holds to a line the studio states plainly: clarity and reduction, and an appropriate balance of tradition and modernity. Craft, site, and sustainability are part of the architecture, not a finish applied later. The houses are spare without going cold — glass set against spruce and stone, massing that answers the bank, the weather, and the tide. Matthew Elliott leads the practice from Blue Hill.

Key references: Maine vernacular  ·  clarity and reduction  ·  the working waterfront

Architecture

House Over Water

Blue Hill Bay, Maine  ·  Photography: Trent Bell

Set at the edge of a steep bank above Blue Hill Bay, the house is held to a bipartite mass by the limits of the site. Floor-to-ceiling glass opens the rooms to the water; at high tide the bay comes in beneath the house. ArchDaily lists Corey Papadopoli and Matthew Elliott as lead architects. The project received a 2016 AIA Maine Citation and the People’s Choice award.

Maine coast

One end of the house bears against a stone escarpment, and the whole is turned toward the Atlantic. The studio does not publish this house as a Casco Bay project.

Whitten Architects

Portland, Maine  ·  Rob Whitten

An architecture practice, kept lighter here than the interiors studios. Whitten has designed New England houses from Portland’s Old Port since 1986 — camps, cottages, new homes, and historic renovations — and the work stays residential. Rob Whitten founded the firm and keeps it small enough to stay involved in every project; Russ Tyson and Tom Lane are principals with him. His measure, stated on the studio site, is that a well-designed home should fit its site and context and look as if it has always been there.

Key references: Maine coast  ·  camps and cottages  ·  fit the site

Tara Mangini and Percy Bright have worked from the Catskills since the summer of 2010, designing the houses they live alongside. Retrouvius

Maria Speake and Adam Hills founded Retrouvius in 1993, a salvage yard with a design practice attached — or the other way around. Jonathan Tuckey’s studio works between London and Andermatt, mostly by taking old buildings apart only as far as they need, then putting them back with new timber, lime, and a folk directness. The craft is structural, not decorative. A chalet stays a chalet; it simply learns a larger room.

Atelier Vime

Vallabrègues, Provence  ·  Anthony Watson & Benoît Rauzy

In Vallabrègues, on the Rhône, Anthony Watson and Benoît Rauzy live in an eighteenth-century house that once held Boyer Ltd., a rattan workshop. Reath Design

Los Angeles, CA  ·  Frances Merrill

Frances Merrill founded Reath Design in 2009 after working at Commune Design, and her studio is everything Commune is — but more layered, more personal. Studio 18

Studio Ashby

London

Practice Overview

Colour, Craft, Personal History

Sophie Ashby launched Studio Ashby in 2012 and rapidly established a distinct position among London's younger decorators: intensely personal spaces charged with colour and personal narrative, yet grounded in serious research into craft and material. Projects frequently reference a client's cultural background or family history — rooms that feel as if they grew from a life rather than a brief.

The studio works across private residences, hospitality (Granger & Co., various hotel projects), and cultural institutions. The characteristic visual language: deep rich walls (terracotta, forest green, inky blue), custom rugs designed for each project, considered lighting with shaded pendants and sconces, furniture mixing bespoke commissions with carefully sourced mid-century and antique pieces, and consistently strong artwork integration — many commissions placed directly with artists. Has been an active collaborator with numerous British and global craft studios.

Key references: Contemporary British craft · Indian textile tradition · Colour theory (David Hicks lineage) · Storytelling interiors

Studio 21

Ben Pentreath

London · Dorset

Practice Overview

The English House, Inhabited

Ben Pentreath Ltd. occupies a specific position in British interiors: deeply learned about the English domestic tradition — the country house, the rectory, the London terrace — but never reverent. His interiors layer chintz with plain linens, antique furniture with contemporary ceramics, botanical prints with bold colour. The shop Pentreath & Hall in Bloomsbury extends the aesthetic into an edited selection of objects and fabrics. Work spans Georgian London townhouses, Dorset farmhouses, and projects in America and Europe.

Pentreath studied architecture, and it shows: his rooms are understood three-dimensionally, with particular attention to proportion, joinery detail, and the relationship between inside and garden. Recurrent motifs include panelled libraries in dark green or dark red, loose-covered sofas in ticking or faded floral, flagstone or worn oak underfoot, and walls hung with drawings and prints in casual arrangements.

Key references: English country house tradition · John Fowler · David Hicks · William Morris · Soane

Studio 22

Grenney Greig

London · Tangier

Practice Overview

Maximalist Luxury, Deeply Layered

Rooted in the Colefax & Fowler tradition, Grenney Greig brings an unabashedly rich, pattern-saturated approach to interior decoration. Rooms are understood as accumulations — of fabric, of object, of colour — built over time rather than conceived whole. Tangier provides a second frame of reference: North African pattern, local craft, the medina palette of saffron, indigo, and sienna overlaid on the quintessentially English.

Signature moves include draped curtains with deep pelmets, walls upholstered in fabric or covered floor-to-ceiling in pictures, rooms where every surface carries its own considered object. Light is controlled with shutters and lined curtains rather than engineered from without. The effect is intimate and deliberately unhurried.

Key references: John Fowler · Colefax tradition · Orientalist romanticism · Majorelle · Cecil Beaton

Studio 23

Studio Peregalli

Milan

Practice Overview

Archaeological Luxury

Roberto Peregalli and Laura Sartori Rimini run the most erudite decorating practice currently active. Working from Milan, they produce interiors of extraordinary historical density — rooms in which Venetian plaster, silk damask, painted trompe-l'oeil, lacquered furniture, and layered antiques converge into something simultaneously ancient and alive. Their visual references span Baroque Rome, Biedermeier Vienna, and the grandest moments of European interior history.

Each project begins with research: into the building's period, the client's family and collecting history, regional craft traditions. The resulting rooms are dense with specific knowledge. Peregalli and Sartori Rimini have published two books — The Presence of the Past and The Slow World of Roberto Peregalli — that provide essential reference for anyone drawn to this order of decoration. Frequent collaborations with craftspeople in Venice, Florence, and the Milanese workshops of furniture-makers and fabric houses sustain a level of bespoke execution rarely attempted elsewhere.

Practice Overview

Romantic Eclecticism

Stephen Alesch and Robin Standefer founded Roman & Williams in 2002, bringing a production design sensibility (both had film backgrounds) to interiors. Each project constructs a complete world — a particular era and mood rendered with period-accurate but never pastiche materials. Studio KO

Studio 28

Neri & Hu

Shanghai

Practice Overview

Cultural Compression

Lyndon Neri and Rossana Hu lead the most internationally significant architecture and design practice based in China. Their work compresses multiple cultural strata — Chinese spatial tradition, Modernist structure, and global material language — into buildings and interiors of charged ambiguity. A project by Neri & Hu asks you to navigate rather than simply inhabit: thresholds, screens, courtyards, and layered sight lines create a cinematic spatial experience.

Hospitality is a particular strength: The Waterhouse at South Bund (Shanghai), The Murray Hotel (Hong Kong), and Sukhothai Bangkok demonstrate a consistent approach of working with existing structure — an industrial warehouse, a 1960s government building — and allowing its bones to define the new interior. The material palette is disciplined: exposed concrete, black steel, aged timber, stone, and a few carefully chosen textiles in deep tones. Neri & Hu also design furniture and objects through their brand De La Espada.

London tile company known for encaustic cement tiles with graphic, geometric patterns drawn from Moroccan, Portuguese, and Victorian traditions — but in contemporary colourways and proportions. Plain English

Suffolk-based kitchen company producing hand-built cabinetry in a traditional English idiom — frame-and-panel construction in solid hardwood, painted in a curated palette of chalky, complex colours. Plain English kitchens read as furniture rather than fitted units: pieces with presence, designed around the idea of the kitchen as the central domestic room rather than a functional appendage. Showrooms in London and New York. Frequently specified in houses where period authenticity or artisan quality is non-negotiable. Long lead times; worth planning early.

Specialist Finish

The Lacquer Company

London-based specialist applying traditional Japanese lacquer technique to furniture and architectural surfaces. High-gloss finishes in bespoke colours — from deep aubergine to pale celadon — create surfaces of extraordinary depth and reflectivity. Work ranges from full lacquering of antique chests and screens to bespoke panels, doors, and room-scale applications. The material is genuinely time-consuming and expensive to produce correctly; the result has no convincing imitation. A key resource for accent pieces and feature surfaces in projects where a moment of high-shine luxury is required.

Architectural Paint

Farrow & Ball  &  Little Greene

Farrow & Ball

The defining British architectural paint company. A palette of 132 colours developed through decades of refinement, characterised by their chalky, complex finish — the result of a high pigment-to-binder ratio and traditional double-pigment process. Colours have names rather than numbers. The most referenced palette in British residential and hospitality design. Estate Emulsion for walls, Modern Emulsion for kitchens and bathrooms, Estate Eggshell for woodwork. Note that coverage per litre is lower than commodity paints; two coats reliably required.

farrow-ball.com →

Little Greene

Manchester-based paint company with a palette rooted in historical research — many colours traced to pigments in use during specific periods from the 1400s to the 1960s. The Historical range provides credible alternatives where Farrow & Ball colours feel too familiar or too pale. Slightly better coverage than F&B at comparable quality. Strong for mid-tone greens, dusty roses, and stone colours. Particularly useful for period properties where exact colour archaeology matters. The wallpaper range extends the historical palette into pattern.

littlegreene.com →
