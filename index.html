Studios

New England & American Farmhouse Craft

Houses that keep their memory: Shaker lines, farmhouse bones, and rooms made by hand, where color is a decision and nothing is there by accident.

01

Hendricks Churchill

Sharon, Connecticut

Heide Hendricks and Rafe Churchill work on the New England house as if it were a living thing: keep the bone, repair the breath, and let color do something a little fearless. From Sharon they design, build, and furnish together — Shaker proportion, Greek Revival trim, farmhouse rooms that are modest on purpose and never plain. The decisions endure because they are few, and because each one has a little mischief in it.

Key references: New England farmhouse · Hancock Shaker Village · Greek Revival · local antiques and craft

Architecture · Interiors

Ellsworth

Sharon, Connecticut · 1871 farmhouse

Their own house, admired for years before they bought it: an 1871 farmhouse on what remains of a dairy farm, thirty-three acres of field and wood along a quiet road. The renovation holds to a first impression of the place. Hay is still cut by a neighboring farmer; the barn is workshop, garage, and party room. The book of the house is Our Way Home.

Ellsworth, Sharon, CT  ·  hendrickschurchill.com →

Village Revival

Litchfield County · 1858 Greek Revival

An 1858 Greek Revival in Litchfield County, later given a mansard and a 1915 carriage house, bought first as a speculation and then restored with the client who claimed it. Historic fabric stays; flow is adjusted only where living asked for it. Plum, ochre, silvery blue, and deep green tie rooms that mix antiques with Scandinavian pieces and botanical papers. The carriage house keeps its twenty-five-foot beams.

Village Revival, Litchfield County  ·  hendrickschurchill.com →

The New Farmhouse

Northwestern Connecticut · new construction, net-zero

A new house that refuses to look new. Studied against early twentieth-century vernacular and the buildings at Hancock Shaker Village, it sits below a wooded hillside over fields, gardens, and an orchard. SIPs, reclaimed fittings, solar, geothermal, and rainwater collection carry a net-zero brief. Inside, burnished lime plaster and trim painted in bold contrast — a quiet drama, and only the furniture the rooms actually need. An early collaboration, and the one that showed Heide and Rafe they should work as a single studio.

The New Farmhouse, northwestern Connecticut  ·  hendrickschurchill.com →

02

Workstead

Brooklyn · Charleston · Salisbury, Connecticut

Robert Highsmith and Stefanie Brechbuehler restore old American houses and then furnish them with their own lighting and joinery, so the new work has the same grain as the old. The mood is what they have called Southern modernism and, up north, folk modernism: original floors and moldings left in peace, a few well-made things, nothing streamlined into silence. The rooms feel inhabited because the craft is specific.

Key references: American house restoration · their own lighting and joinery · folk modernism

Interiors · Lighting · Buildings

Workstead House

Bee's Row, Charleston · 1853

An 1853 Italianate row house on Charleston's Bee's Row, restored with its original floors, moldings, doors, windows, and stair, and then tuned — their phrase — as Southern modernism. Brick left bare where it wanted to be, new light and furniture sitting easily against the nineteenth-century shell. A pied-à-terre and a place to gather, not a museum of the Old South.

Workstead House, Charleston  ·  workstead.com →

Ingleside

Salisbury, Connecticut · restored 2020–2024

The founders' own house, on a hill in Salisbury's historic district. Parts of the structure reach into the eighteenth century; most of it was built in 1815. Tall 12-over-12 windows, a knot garden, a meadow, a long stone wall. Antique and new furniture share the rooms. The 1856 carriage house was rebuilt as a studio — clapboard, cedar roof, a concrete floor, a loft — with the old use still legible in the stair.

Ingleside, Salisbury, CT  ·  workstead.com →

03

Jersey Ice Cream Co.

Catskills · Hudson Valley, New York

Tara Mangini and Percy Bright founded the studio in 2010 — the name from a flea-market embosser, not a recipe — and for years they lived inside the houses they were remaking. Percy trowels the plaster and builds the furniture; Tara gathers the old things and the color. The work is hand-troweled walls, reclaimed wood, and rooms that feel mended rather than decorated. Careful, a little magic, never fussy.

Key references: hand-troweled plaster · reclaimed wood · self-made furniture · Arts & Crafts houses

Interior Design

Minwawa

Tannersville, New York · Arts & Crafts

Their studio dates this mountain house to 1887: an Arts & Crafts place in the artist colony Candace Wheeler helped found above Tannersville, later published in Architectural Digest. They came in after the structure was put right and worked with a light hand — original detail left to lead, plaster and vintage furniture brought up to the present without erasing the time warp. Lovell Kuhn Construction did the bones.

Minwawa, Tannersville, NY  ·  jerseyicecreamco.com →

Their upstate farmhouse

Upstate New York · 1870

After years of moving from job to job, they bought a rundown 1870 farmhouse and stayed. Country Living records the method: live in the dust, let the house decide. Plaster — Percy's long study — on the walls and the range hood, original floors refinished, cabinetry in reclaimed hemlock, tempera paint with the milky depth that suits gypsum. Swedish farmhouse color, learned on a trip, for a long New York winter. Photographs courtesy of the studio.

Their farmhouse, upstate New York  ·  Country Living  ·  photographs courtesy of Jersey Ice Cream Co.

04

Heidi Caillier Design

Seattle

Heidi Caillier builds rooms that feel as if a family had already been living in them for a century — cottage scale, layered pattern, vintage sitting next to something made last year. New houses are the harder trick, and she treats them as such: how to keep a just-built room from looking just-built. The restraint is in the editing. The magic is that the rooms still seem to have a plot.

Key references: American cottage · collected pattern · houses that feel older than they are

Interior Design

Colorado ranch

Southwest Colorado · new build, 2025

A young family on thirteen acres began with a 1970s farmhouse and, after the plan and a growing household made renovation the wrong tool, built again — asking for a house that felt as if it had stood there for generations. Shingled roof, earth colors, the landscape doing half the work. Architectural Digest published it in March 2025.

Colorado ranch  ·  Photography: Haris Kenjar  ·  Architectural Digest, 2025  ·  heidicaillierdesign.com →

Mill Valley

Mill Valley, California · 1896

An 1896 fishing retreat — walls once insulated with the San Francisco Chronicle — for a Marin family who asked for a house of Mill Valley, not a style. Paint and paper give the open plan its rooms. A thread of Sea Ranch runs through a white-oak guesthouse. Frederic published the house in 2025; the same photographer.

Mill Valley, California  ·  Photography: Haris Kenjar  ·  heidicaillierdesign.com →

Katie Martinez Design

San Francisco · New England

KMD blends playful and refined — layering vintage, handcrafted, and custom-designed pieces with a sensitivity to color and pattern that feels collected rather than decorated. Her bathrooms use artisan tile, warm wood cabinetry, and natural stone to create spaces that feel both personal and timeless. The perfect balance of quiet and quirky.

Mother Studio

Asheville, NC  ·  Working Nationally

Laura Evans and Karie Reinertson run a studio grounded in the culture of the handmade. Bespoke hand-painted tile, wood-paneled walls, and an easy interplay of pattern and natural material define bathrooms that feel rooted in craft and place. Nothing obvious. Nothing cheap. Salvage, Patina & Adaptive Reuse

Old material, given another life — timber, plaster, and the odd rescued thing — arranged so the building feels found rather than finished.

07

Retrouvius

London

Maria Speake and Adam Hills began in Glasgow in 1993, appalled at the joinery being thrown out of tenements, and built a London practice in which salvage is the material, not the mood. Adam keeps the warehouse; Maria's studio puts the orphaned parts back to work — with wit, and with screws rather than glue, so a thing can move again. The rooms are thrifty and slightly enchanted. Nothing is over-finished.

Key references: architectural salvage · limewash and timber · reuse as the design method

Salvage · Interior Design

Country Home in the City

Notting Hill, London

A townhouse just off Portobello, for a costume designer who wanted the quiet of a country house. Baked earth, limewash, eroded stone, antique textiles. Eighteenth-century parquet becomes kitchen cabinet fronts; a seventeenth-century fireplace reads as if it had always been in the snug; a guest bed is lined in reclaimed pine cheeseboards. The house was meant to feel as though it had always looked like this.

Country Home in the City, London  ·  retrouvius.com →

The Garages

Kensal Rise, London · their home and studio

A plot of breeze-block garages, now their house and laboratory. The council required a single dwelling; they built a sunken courtyard reached by a tunnel, and furnished it from the leftovers — Lutyens teak as sliding partitions, Patent Office shelving as flooring, cheeseboards on a ceiling, a suede-panelled kitchen. World of Interiors published it in October 2025 as "Remains to Be Seen."

The Garages, Kensal Rise  ·  Photography: Simon Upton  ·  World of Interiors, October 2025  ·  retrouvius.com →

08

Tuckey Design Studio

London · Andermatt

Jonathan Tuckey's studio works on the affection people already have for a building, then makes that building usable for another generation. Adaptive reuse is the usual path; new work, when it comes, is held to the same standard of craft. In the Alps the palette is timber, lime, and a folk touch that never turns cute — balconies, dormers, a stair that still knows the cattle once lived downstairs.

Key references: adaptive reuse · crafted timber · alpine vernacular · folk detail

Architecture

Halbhaus

Andermatt, Switzerland · 17th-century chalet

A listed chalet, repaired rather than replaced. A double-height void lifts the old agricultural ceiling; bedrooms sit in an insulated attic; a spa is cut into the rock under the original stair. New timber shingles let the elevation sit among its neighbors. Heating comes from a communal wood-chip boiler. The timber already in the building is kept, and asked to do new work.

Halbhaus, Andermatt  ·  Photography: Dario Borruto  ·  tuckeydesign.com →

Nossenhaus

Andermatt · building of 1620

The second-oldest building in Andermatt, from 1620: families above, animals below, fallen into disrepair. With Ruedi Kreienbühl it is now three apartments and a wine bar. Stone to the weather, timber to the sun, the two locked together as they once were. Lime plaster, larch, and pine were chosen to fade into the old fabric. New pine balconies and zinc dormers give the outside a playful face without abandoning the village.

Nossenhaus, Andermatt  ·  Photography: James Brittain  ·  tuckeydesign.com →

09

Roman & Williams

New York

Practice Overview

Romantic Eclecticism

Stephen Alesch and Robin Standefer founded Roman & Williams in 2002, bringing a production design sensibility (both had film backgrounds) to interiors. Each project constructs a complete world — a particular era and mood rendered with period-accurate but never pastiche materials. Their most celebrated work balances Victorian-era sourcing (pressed tin ceilings, salvaged brick, cast iron) with modernist furniture and contemporary art; the result is romantic without being sentimental.

The Guild, their Paris shop on the Île Saint-Louis, is a landmark reference point for how antiques, contemporary craft, and design objects coexist at retail scale. Key projects include The Standard Hotel New York, Le Coucou restaurant, Atera, and an extensive body of residential work in New York, the Hamptons, and internationally. Their own house in Connecticut has been widely published as a master class in romantic American country.

Key references: Victorian American vernacular · Arts & Crafts · Film set design · Craft object collecting

Green River Project

Brooklyn

Aaron Aujla and Benjamin Bloomstein started the studio in 2017 after a decade of making work as artists in New York. Furniture and rooms are researched the way a story is researched: a material, a place, a use, then the object. Private houses sit beside the yearly collections. The finish is rarely polite. Wood keeps its marks; the room feels as if it had been told something and remembered it.

Key references: artist-made furniture · narrative collections · raw timber and cloth

Quiet rooms with a pulse: plaster, linen, stone, and light, held back just far enough that the atmosphere can do the work.

11

Studioilse

London  ·  Ilse Crawford

Ilse Crawford is among the most original design thinkers of her generation — a studio that consistently asks what spaces are for, how they feel to inhabit, and what it means to be human within them. The work moves across scales and typologies: private homes, floating residences, rural coaching inns, Stockholm guest houses. What binds them is a commitment to sensory intelligence over visual novelty, and a deep literacy in materials, craft, and the domestic arts. She is equally at home with hand-stitched textiles and structural timber as with contemporary furniture or hand-painted antiques. Old and new in conversation, never in conflict.

Interior Design · Architecture · Strategy

Chalet, Switzerland

Switzerland  ·  2015  ·  Photography: Felix Odell

A family mountain home buried deep in the Swiss Alps. Working from a warm palette of natural materials — pitched timber, stone, hand-stitched textiles — Studioilse created a chalet that can adapt from festive gathering to quiet hibernation. A hand-painted botanical chest in the bedroom demonstrates the studio's belief in the singular object: one piece that anchors an entire room and earns its place through character, not price.

Chalet, Swiss Alps, 2015  ·  Photography: Felix Odell  ·  studioilse.com →

Shemara, Motor Yacht

Mallorca  ·  2015  ·  Photography: Casper Sejersen

The refurbishment of a historic motor yacht originally built in 1938. Studioilse's brief was to create a floating family home that made sense for both owners and crew — evoking the glamorous spirit of the boat's extraordinary history without becoming a slave to it. The result is a yacht interior that feels genuinely domestic: proper rooms, not cabins. The Shemara's story includes the Titanic; its interior should acknowledge history without pastiche.

Shemara, Motor Yacht, 2015  ·  Photography: Casper Sejersen  ·  studioilse.com →

Ett Hem, Number 4

Stockholm, Sweden  ·  2022  ·  Photography: Magnus Marding

A decade after Studioilse helped define a new language of hospitality with the original Ett Hem, they returned to expand the Stockholm guest house into the house next door. The same values, a new building: intimate and lively, domestic and public at once. Generous light from large windows opens onto rooftop views. Food and the local supply chain are foregrounded. The rooms are expansive but never grand — they feel like somewhere you might actually want to live.

Ett Hem, Number 4, Stockholm, 2022  ·  Photography: Magnus Marding  ·  studioilse.com →

The Olde Bell Inn

Hurley, Berkshire  ·  2007  ·  Photography: Lisa Cohen

A benchmark project: the contemporary reinterpretation of a traditional English coaching inn. Forty-seven bedrooms, a dining room, bar, malt house, and meadow garden — all reinterpreted using British materials, furniture, and local produce. The result is a rural hospitality model rooted in local life, not heritage pastiche. Old and new placed confidently side by side: exposed beams meet contemporary upholstery; antique mirrors hold company with clean-lined lighting. Craft, not costume.

The Olde Bell Inn, Hurley, Berkshire, 2007  ·  Photography: Lisa Cohen  ·  studioilse.com →

12

Rose Uniacke

London, Pimlico  ·  roseuniacke.com

Rose Uniacke is among the most consequential British interior designers of the last twenty years — a figure who has largely redefined what domestic elegance looks like in the contemporary era. Her signature is "sumptuous minimalism": period rooms approached with a pared-back choreography, where antiques from antiquity to the 20th century live alongside bespoke contemporary pieces and textiles. Light and proportion are paramount. Art — often by female artists working in textile, ceramics, and paper — is integral, not decorative afterthought. Her own fabric and paint lines extend the practice into a complete material ecosystem. Recent work includes London townhouses, a New York hotel (San Vicente West Village), the Belmond Royal Scotsman, and her personal Coach House — photographed by Francois Halard for World of Interiors.

Interior Design · Fabric · Paint · Antiques

San Vicente West Village  ·  New York  ·  Photography: Simon Upton

A Place with a Colorful History

A comprehensive interior renovation of a 1908 redbrick building in Manhattan's West Village — once part of the Jane Hotel, originally lodgings for visiting sailors. Rose Uniacke transformed it into a members' club and hotel for Jeff Klein: a quiet, layered sophistication grounded in the building's bones. As written in Architectural Digest: "Walking into a room by Rose feels like walking into the best version of the home you wish you owned."

13

Axel Vervoordt

Antwerp · Wijnegem · S'Gravenwezel

Practice Overview

Wabi & Timelessness

Axel Vervoordt Company has operated from the Castle of 't Steen near Antwerp for decades, producing interiors that translate Japanese wabi-sabi philosophy into the European context. The aesthetic is one of disciplined restraint: raw plaster, aged timber, natural stone, linen, and hand-thrown ceramics — all in a palette of ash, straw, and faded earth. Antiques and contemporary art sit alongside each other without hierarchy, unified by the quality of material and the hand of time.

Vervoordt is also a significant gallerist and furniture dealer; the boundary between the shop, the showroom, and the design practice is deliberately porous. Hospitality commissions (Les Ottomans Istanbul, RFH Le Royal Monceau Paris) demonstrate that the approach scales to the institutional. The annual TEFAF booth has become a defining reference for how antique and contemporary objects can coexist without apology. His son Boris now leads the firm.

Key references: Wabi-sabi · Mingei movement · Axel's own art dealing practice · Mies van der Rohe restraint

14

Atelier Vime

Vallabrègues, Provence

Anthony Watson and Benoît Rauzy restored an eighteenth-century house on the Rhône and found, in the dust, that it had been a wicker workshop in a village that once wove for much of France. They replanted the willow, hired the remaining makers, and let limewash, reed, and old ceramics set the rooms. The result is not a show of craft. It is a house that remembered its trade and became generous about it. Vime, they note, is the Latin for flexible wood — osier.

Key references: limewash · Provençal wicker · eighteenth-century town house · revived local craft

Vallabrègues · eighteenth century

The hôtel particulier is their home. Next door, a former workshop they later bought is La Maison Vime, a seasonal shop set up as rooms you can walk through — new and old rattan, local ceramics, books. Summer salons in yellow limewash, courtyards left to weather. Remodelista's photographs of the shop are published courtesy of the studio; a single photographer is not named.

15

Vincent Van Duysen

Antwerp, Belgium

Practice Overview

Serene Materiality

Vincent Van Duysen Architects produces what is arguably the most refined residential architecture-and-interiors practice working in Belgium today. His projects — often in converted farmhouses, industrial buildings, or newly built villas — are defined by monolithic calm: plaster, stone, concrete, linen, and dark-stained timber deployed in massive, unhurried compositions. Light enters in long horizontal bands; surfaces wear their age; rooms breathe.

Van Duysen was creative director of Molteni&C (2016–22), translating the studio aesthetic into furniture at scale. The furniture is as important as the architecture: his pieces for Molteni, RH, and various collaborators share the same interest in mass, proportion, and material patina. A project by VVD invariably uses his own furniture alongside antiques — a bed frame in oxidised brass, a sofa in undyed linen, a dining table in solid travertine — making the whole indivisible from the parts.

Key references: Minimalism with warmth · Belgian vernacular · John Pawson discipline · Axel Vervoordt restraint · Molteni&C collaboration

Reath Design

Los Angeles, CA  ·  Frances Merrill

Frances Merrill founded Reath Design in 2009 after working at Commune Design, and her studio is everything Commune is — but more layered, more personal. She uses wallpaper with abandon, mixes pattern and texture across eras, and brings an art-world sensibility that keeps rooms from tipping into decoration. Her bathrooms are known for a strong material gesture — graphic tile, a slab of book-matched marble, warm wood — held in tension with something unexpected: a vintage fixture, an odd color, a print that shouldn't work but does. Quiet confidence with a wink.

Nickey Kehoe

Todd Nickey and Amy Kehoe run one of LA's most quietly distinctive studios — color-forward without being obvious, eclectic without being chaotic. Studio Ashby

London

Practice Overview

Colour, Craft, Personal History

Sophie Ashby launched Studio Ashby in 2012 and rapidly established a distinct position among London's younger decorators: intensely personal spaces charged with colour and personal narrative, yet grounded in serious research into craft and material. Projects frequently reference a client's cultural background or family history — rooms that feel as if they grew from a life rather than a brief.

The studio works across private residences, hospitality (Granger & Co., various hotel projects), and cultural institutions. The characteristic visual language: deep rich walls (terracotta, forest green, inky blue), custom rugs designed for each project, considered lighting with shaded pendants and sconces, furniture mixing bespoke commissions with carefully sourced mid-century and antique pieces, and consistently strong artwork integration — many commissions placed directly with artists. Has been an active collaborator with numerous British and global craft studios.

Key references: Contemporary British craft · Indian textile tradition · Colour theory (David Hicks lineage) · Storytelling interiors

Arent&Pyke

Sydney & Melbourne, Australia  ·  Juliette Arent & Sarah-Jane Pyke

Juliette Arent and Sarah-Jane Pyke are among the most decorated interior designers in Australia and have become genuine international figures — regulars in World of Interiors, Vogue Living, and Elle Decoration, with a monograph published by Thames & Hudson. Their work is defined by an extraordinary layering of material, art, object, and memory: rooms that feel genuinely inhabited by specific people with specific pasts. Vintage finds and design classics appear alongside custom fabrication; color is used with restraint and confidence simultaneously. Their bathrooms are benchmark work — arched stone frames, freestanding baths, joinery floating above stone floors, custom marble islands. Nothing accidental; everything thought through in minute detail.

Studio Valle de Valle

Ben Pentreath

London · Dorset

Practice Overview

The English House, Inhabited

Ben Pentreath Ltd. occupies a specific position in British interiors: deeply learned about the English domestic tradition — the country house, the rectory, the London terrace — but never reverent. His interiors layer chintz with plain linens, antique furniture with contemporary ceramics, botanical prints with bold colour. The shop Pentreath & Hall in Bloomsbury extends the aesthetic into an edited selection of objects and fabrics. Work spans Georgian London townhouses, Dorset farmhouses, and projects in America and Europe.

Pentreath studied architecture, and it shows: his rooms are understood three-dimensionally, with particular attention to proportion, joinery detail, and the relationship between inside and garden. Recurrent motifs include panelled libraries in dark green or dark red, loose-covered sofas in ticking or faded floral, flagstone or worn oak underfoot, and walls hung with drawings and prints in casual arrangements.

Key references: English country house tradition · John Fowler · David Hicks · William Morris · Soane

Max Rollitt

Hampshire, England

Max Rollitt is a decorator, a furniture maker, and a dealer, all of it under one roof at Yavington Barn, a former grain store in the Hampshire fields. Grenney Greig

London · Tangier

Practice Overview

Maximalist Luxury, Deeply Layered

Rooted in the Colefax & Fowler tradition, Grenney Greig brings an unabashedly rich, pattern-saturated approach to interior decoration. Rooms are understood as accumulations — of fabric, of object, of colour — built over time rather than conceived whole. Tangier provides a second frame of reference: North African pattern, local craft, the medina palette of saffron, indigo, and sienna overlaid on the quintessentially English.

Signature moves include draped curtains with deep pelmets, walls upholstered in fabric or covered floor-to-ceiling in pictures, rooms where every surface carries its own considered object. Light is controlled with shutters and lined curtains rather than engineered from without. The effect is intimate and deliberately unhurried.

Key references: John Fowler · Colefax tradition · Orientalist romanticism · Majorelle · Cecil Beaton

Studio Peregalli

Milan

Practice Overview

Archaeological Luxury

Roberto Peregalli and Laura Sartori Rimini run the most erudite decorating practice currently active. Working from Milan, they produce interiors of extraordinary historical density — rooms in which Venetian plaster, silk damask, painted trompe-l'oeil, lacquered furniture, and layered antiques converge into something simultaneously ancient and alive. Their visual references span Baroque Rome, Biedermeier Vienna, and the grandest moments of European interior history.

Each project begins with research: into the building's period, the client's family and collecting history, regional craft traditions. The resulting rooms are dense with specific knowledge. Peregalli and Sartori Rimini have published two books — The Presence of the Past and The Slow World of Roberto Peregalli — that provide essential reference for anyone drawn to this order of decoration. Frequent collaborations with craftspeople in Venice, Florence, and the Milanese workshops of furniture-makers and fabric houses sustain a level of bespoke execution rarely attempted elsewhere.

Key references: Renzo Mongiardino · Alvar Aalto · Biedermeier · Venetian Baroque · Trompe-l'oeil tradition

Studio KO

Paris · Marrakech · London  ·  Karl Fournier & Olivier Marty

Karl Fournier and Olivier Marty founded Studio KO in Paris in 1999 and have since become one of the most internationally admired practices of their generation — moving between rigorous Moroccan villas, London hospitality (Chiltern Firehouse), the Yves Saint Laurent Museum in Marrakech, boutiques for Aesop and Ami, and private residences across France, Morocco, Corsica, and Los Angeles. Their work holds a generative tension: "constantly torn between our desire for extremely pared-down, pure things and a more decorative, busy aesthetic." The common denominator is that their buildings are always completely in tune with their environment — materials, handicraft, landscape. Multiple features in World of Interiors.

Joseph Dirand

Paris

Practice Overview

Haussmann Rigour

Joseph Dirand Architecture operates in the gap between interior design and architecture — each project begins from structure, moves through proportion, and arrives at an interior of almost Baroque control. Parisian apartments are the central subject: enfilades of white rooms with high moulded ceilings, chevron parquet floors, and an extremely spare selection of objects chosen for sculptural silhouette rather than period provenance.

The palette is rigorously restrained — usually warm white or off-white walls, honey-toned stone, bleached or dark-stained wood — but the detail work is elaborate: bespoke joinery, custom light fixtures in plaster or blackened steel, upholstery in Dedar or Hermès fabrics. Hospitality projects (Monsieur Bleu Paris, Girafe Paris, Cecconi's) prove the approach can fill a very large room without losing intimacy. High reference for anyone working on Haussmann-era renovations.

Key references: French classical proportion · Surrealist object · Pierre Paulin furniture · Paris apartment tradition

Neri & Hu

Shanghai

Practice Overview

Cultural Compression

Lyndon Neri and Rossana Hu lead the most internationally significant architecture and design practice based in China. Their work compresses multiple cultural strata — Chinese spatial tradition, Modernist structure, and global material language — into buildings and interiors of charged ambiguity. A project by Neri & Hu asks you to navigate rather than simply inhabit: thresholds, screens, courtyards, and layered sight lines create a cinematic spatial experience.

Hospitality is a particular strength: The Waterhouse at South Bund (Shanghai), The Murray Hotel (Hong Kong), and Sukhothai Bangkok demonstrate a consistent approach of working with existing structure — an industrial warehouse, a 1960s government building — and allowing its bones to define the new interior. The material palette is disciplined: exposed concrete, black steel, aged timber, stone, and a few carefully chosen textiles in deep tones. Neri & Hu also design furniture and objects through their brand De La Espada.

Key references: Chinese courtyard spatial logic · Japanese craft · Brutalist structure · De La Espada furniture

Matthew Elliott's office in Blue Hill designs houses and a few institutional buildings, most of them on the Maine coast and islands. Products & Makers

Tiles · Stone · Ceramics · Paint · Kitchens

A working reference for materials and makers that recur across projects of character and craft. Each of these suppliers operates at a level of specificity — in texture, colour variation, or production method — that distinguishes them from commodity alternatives.

Ceramic Tile

Dzek

London-based tile company operating at the intersection of contemporary art and industrial ceramic process. Founded on the idea that the most compelling surfaces are not reproductions but new things with depth. Collaborations with artists including Bethan Laura Wood, Fernando Mastrangelo, and Tauba Auerbach have produced encaustic, terrazzo, and bas-relief tiles with genuine visual specificity. High reference for statement floors and feature walls.

dzek.co.uk →

Stone & Tile

Lapicida

Yorkshire-based specialists in natural stone, marble, and bespoke tile. Lapicida operates its own quarrying operations and stoneworking workshops, enabling a level of control over material, finish, and custom cutting that is genuinely rare. Extensive archive of antique and reclaimed stone. Frequently specified for bathroom floors, kitchen flags, and period-appropriate flooring in listed buildings. Known for working closely with architects and decorators from early design stage through installation.

lapicida.com →

Encaustic & Terracotta Tile

Bert & May

London tile company known for encaustic cement tiles with graphic, geometric patterns drawn from Moroccan, Portuguese, and Victorian traditions — but in contemporary colourways and proportions. Their tiles have become ubiquitous in a certain register of restaurant and residential project because they accomplish something rare: a floor that immediately reads as considered, without being loud. The material itself — unsealed cement, matte surface — ages gracefully, accumulating patina rather than wear.

bertandmay.com →

Furniture & Objects

Pinch Design

London furniture and homeware studio founded by Russell Pinch and Oona Bannon. Pieces are designed and made in the UK in solid hardwoods — oak, walnut, ash — with a sensibility that lands between Scandinavian functionalism and English Arts & Crafts: warm, honest, quietly proportioned. The Orson sofa, Button chair, and various storage pieces have become staples in projects where the furniture needs to be present without dominating. Collaborations with selected fabric studios; upholstery available in a wide range.

pinchdesign.com →

Bespoke Kitchens

Plain English

Suffolk-based kitchen company producing hand-built cabinetry in a traditional English idiom — frame-and-panel construction in solid hardwood, painted in a curated palette of chalky, complex colours. Plain English kitchens read as furniture rather than fitted units: pieces with presence, designed around the idea of the kitchen as the central domestic room rather than a functional appendage. Showrooms in London and New York. Frequently specified in houses where period authenticity or artisan quality is non-negotiable. Long lead times; worth planning early.

plainenglishdesign.co.uk →

Specialist Finish

The Lacquer Company

London-based specialist applying traditional Japanese lacquer technique to furniture and architectural surfaces. High-gloss finishes in bespoke colours — from deep aubergine to pale celadon — create surfaces of extraordinary depth and reflectivity. Work ranges from full lacquering of antique chests and screens to bespoke panels, doors, and room-scale applications. The material is genuinely time-consuming and expensive to produce correctly; the result has no convincing imitation. A key resource for accent pieces and feature surfaces in projects where a moment of high-shine luxury is required.

thelacquercompany.com →

Architectural Paint

Farrow & Ball  &  Little Greene

Farrow & Ball

The defining British architectural paint company. A palette of 132 colours developed through decades of refinement, characterised by their chalky, complex finish — the result of a high pigment-to-binder ratio and traditional double-pigment process. Colours have names rather than numbers. The most referenced palette in British residential and hospitality design. Estate Emulsion for walls, Modern Emulsion for kitchens and bathrooms, Estate Eggshell for woodwork. Note that coverage per litre is lower than commodity paints; two coats reliably required.

farrow-ball.com →

Little Greene

Manchester-based paint company with a palette rooted in historical research — many colours traced to pigments in use during specific periods from the 1400s to the 1960s. The Historical range provides credible alternatives where Farrow & Ball colours feel too familiar or too pale. Slightly better coverage than F&B at comparable quality. Strong for mid-tone greens, dusty roses, and stone colours. Particularly useful for period properties where exact colour archaeology matters. The wallpaper range extends the historical palette into pattern.

littlegreene.com →
